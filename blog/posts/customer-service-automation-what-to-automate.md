Most conversations about customer service automation start and end with chatbots. That's a shame, because the chatbot is usually the least valuable part. The bigger wins are behind the scenes, in the work agents do around every conversation: sorting, looking things up, logging, chasing other teams, and preparing cases for someone to approve.

Here's how we break down a service desk when we look at it for automation.

## The work around the conversation

Watch an agent handle a single "where's my order?" email and count the steps:

1. Open the email and work out what it's about
2. Find the customer in the CRM
3. Find the order in the ERP or order system
4. Check the shipment status in the carrier's portal
5. Write the reply
6. Log the contact, pick a reason code, close the ticket

Only step 5 involves talking to the customer. The other five are lookups and data entry across three or four systems. That's the pattern across most of the service desk, and it's where automation does the most.

## What to automate

### Sorting and routing

Reading each incoming request, working out what it's about and who should handle it. This is high volume, it has to happen before anything else, and AI is good at it. See [ticket triage automation](post.html?slug=customer-service-ticket-triage-automation).

### Lookups

Order status, delivery tracking, invoice copies, account balances, warranty dates. The answer exists in a system somewhere. A bot or integration can fetch it and attach it to the ticket, so the agent (or an automated reply) has it immediately.

### Routine replies

For request types where the answer is a lookup and the customer just needs the information, automated replies can go out in minutes. Start with drafts for agents to approve, then let the most reliable types send on their own. See [automating the shared inbox](post.html?slug=automating-the-customer-service-shared-inbox).

### Logging and wrap-up

Contact records, summaries, reason codes and follow-up tasks, written from the conversation itself. See [automating ticket logging](post.html?slug=automate-ticket-logging-and-crm-updates).

### Chasing

SLA warnings, reminders to internal teams, follow-ups when a customer hasn't replied, and pulling tickets back when an answer arrives. See [SLA monitoring and escalation](post.html?slug=sla-monitoring-and-escalation-automation).

### Case preparation

For returns, refunds, credits and disputes, the automation gathers everything a person needs to decide: the order, the invoice, the payment, the customer's history, the relevant policy. The agent or manager approves, and the approved action (raising a credit note, booking a return) can be carried out automatically.

### Updates between systems

Address changes, contact updates, order amendments and account notes that need to be copied from the ticket into the CRM or ERP. Where the change is sensitive, like bank details or a delivery address on a high-value order, the automation prepares it and a person confirms it.

## What to keep human

- **Complaints.** Customers who are upset want to know a person heard them.
- **Judgment calls.** Goodwill gestures, exceptions to policy, anything where "it depends".
- **Key accounts.** The relationship is the product.
- **Disputes about money.** Price, quality or delivery disputes need someone who can negotiate.
- **Anything new.** The first few times a new type of problem appears, a person should handle it and decide how it should be handled going forward.

## A note on chatbots

Customer-facing bots can work well for narrow, well-defined requests, like order status, if they're connected to real data. They work badly when they're used to keep customers away from people. If you add one, make sure it can see the same systems your agents can, and that getting to a person is easy.

In our experience, automating the work behind the desk tends to pay back faster than a chatbot in front of it, and customers notice the result anyway: faster, more accurate answers from people who aren't rushed.

## Where to start

Pick the request type with the most volume and the least judgment. For most B2B and e-commerce service teams, that's order status or document requests. Automate triage and the lookup for that one type, measure it, then add the next.
